0.1

Welcome

You'll build a robot from a shipping box and your ELEGOO kit that keeps a ping-pong ball wherever you point, on a track that tilts. Nobody writes its balancing rules. A small neural network learns them by reinforcement learning in a simulator you can watch, the Gym. You then send that brain down the USB cable into the Arduino and see whether what it learned in the dream holds up in reality.

Everything here was tested before you build it: the firmware was run on a computer against the simulated robot, calibration was tried on hundreds of deliberately crooked simulated builds, and the brain you'll train beats a hand-tuned classic controller. What I couldn't test is your actual cardboard. That's the experiment.

0.2

How it works

The trick that makes a dream-trained brain survive reality is domain randomization. Every practice run, the simulator re-rolls the robot's quirks: how sticky the track is, how slow and sloppy the servo is, how noisy the sonar is, how crooked "level" is. A brain that balances in all of those worlds has a good chance in yours.

Part 1

Build the beam

All cardboard. Hot glue sets in about a minute; hold each joint square until it does. The end with the sensor is "the sonar end" and is at 0 in on every measurement.

1.1

Cut the cardboard

Open them on GitHub

The same file is guide/fulcrum-cut-templates.pdf in the fulcrum folder.

  1. Cut the base, 20½ × 6¼ in.
  2. Cut the track strip, 17¼ × 2¾ in, with the corrugation (the ridges inside the cardboard) running the 17¼ in way. That keeps it straight.
  3. Print the templates, cut them out, trace onto cardboard, and cut: 4 saddles, 2 towers, 2 tower braces, the sonar plate, the far wall, the servo post and its brace.

Clean cuts: several light passes with a sharp blade against a metal ruler beat one hard push. Cut on something you don't mind scratching.

1.2

Fold the track

  1. Draw a line down the middle of the track strip, 1⅜ in from each long edge.
  2. Score it: press the back of a butter knife (or a dry ballpoint) firmly along the line, against a ruler.
  3. Fold along the score into a V with a right angle, score on the outside.
  4. Optional: run clear packing tape down the inside of each face for a smoother, more even roll.
1.3

Saddles and straw

  1. On the outside of the V, mark across it at 2, 8⅝, 11⅜ and 15⅜ in from the sonar end.
  2. Cut a straw to 4⅛ in and glue it along the bottom edge of one saddle, centered. That's the pivot saddle.
  3. In a second saddle, poke a small hole ¼ in above its bottom edge, centered, with a skewer tip. That's the push-rod saddle.
  4. Glue the saddles on the marks with their notches hugging the V: pivot saddle at 8⅝, push-rod saddle at 11⅜, the plain ones at 2 and 15⅜. The straw runs across the track.
1.4

Sonar plate and far wall

  1. Hold the sonar plate across the sonar end, set the ball in the V against it, and mark the plate at the height of the ball's center.
  2. Draw two ⅝ in circles centered ½ in left and right of the mark (1 in apart), and cut them out. The sensor's silver cans should just fit.
  3. Glue the plate across the end, centered on the V, its bottom edge about ⅜ in below the fold.
  4. Glue the far wall across the other end.
1.5

Towers

  1. Poke a skewer hole in each tower, ⅝ in below the top, centered.
  2. Draw a line across the base at its middle (10¼ in from either end). Glue the towers upright on that line, facing each other, 4¼ in apart (inside faces), centered across the base.
  3. Before the glue sets, slide a skewer through both holes so they line up.
  4. Glue a triangle brace against the inside face of each tower, along the edge nearest the sonar end. (On the outside they'd hang off the base.)
1.6

Hang the beam

  1. With the sonar end on your left, push a skewer through one tower, through the straw, and out the other tower.
  2. Nudge the beam. It should rock freely and settle without sticking. If it rubs, trim the straw.
  3. Trim the skewer so about ½ in sticks out past each tower.

The servo goes on in Part 4, once the electronics have been tested.

Part 2

Wire the electronics

Do this on the table, in front of the robot near the sonar end, with nothing plugged into your Mac or the wall. In each diagram the new wires are in full color and earlier ones are faded. Any hole on the correct rail works; the diagrams show a tidy layout that keeps everything in the left half of the board.

2.1

Power

  1. Press the Power Supply Module onto the left end of the breadboard: its − pins on the blue stripes, + pins on the red stripes, DC jack facing out.
  2. Top jumper on 5V. Bottom jumper on OFF.
  3. Red jumper: Arduino 5V → bottom red (+) rail. Black jumper: Arduino GND → bottom blue (−) rail.
  4. Black jumper: top blue rail → bottom blue rail, so both supplies share a ground.

Why two supplies: the servo pulls sudden bursts of current that would jolt the sensors' readings. The top rail (power module) feeds only the servo; the bottom rail (the Arduino's own 5 V) feeds everything else.

Part 3

Bring it to life

Everything from here on happens in the Gym, a web app in the fulcrum folder on your Mac. It has three tabs: Dream (training), Exam (testing) and Robot (the USB connection).

3.1

Open the Gym

  1. Open the Gym in Chrome at michaellomuscio.github.io/fulcrum/gym. It can reach the Arduino over USB from there.
  2. Or run it from your Mac, which also works offline: in Finder, open the fulcrum folder and double-click Fulcrum Gym.command. Chrome shows the Gym at localhost:8642/gym/; keep the small Terminal window open while you work. (Not on a Mac? Run python3 -m http.server 8642 in the folder.)

USB needs Chrome or Edge; Safari and Firefox don't support it. A Gym opened from inside claude.ai can train, run exams and drive a virtual robot, but can't reach USB.

3.2

Install the firmware

  1. Plug the Arduino into your Mac (through the USB-C adapter).
  2. In the Robot tab, click Connect and pick the port named like usbmodem… or usbserial….
  3. Click Install firmware. About ten seconds later Fulcrum beeps twice and the status line says hello.

Plan B, the Arduino IDE: open fulcrum/firmware/Fulcrum/Fulcrum.ino, choose Tools › Board › Arduino Uno and your port, then Upload. No extra libraries are needed. Or flash it from Terminal with arduino-cli (see the README).

3.3

Test every part

  1. Plug the 9 V adapter into the power module and press its button (its LED lights).
  2. Wave a hand over the sonar: the reading changes. The gyro says found. Turn the knob end to end. Press any remote button.
  3. Click Beep and Light every LED.
  4. Click Tilt toward sensor, Level and Tilt away: the servo's shaft turns.

Something silent? See Troubleshooting. Fixing it now, with everything on the table, is much easier than later.

Part 4

Finish the robot

Mount the servo while the firmware holds it at its exact middle, then move the sensors onto the beam and let Fulcrum calibrate itself.

4.1

Servo and push-rod

  1. Click Level in the Gym so the servo holds its middle position.
  2. Glue the servo post to the base right behind the push-rod saddle (11⅜ in), its front face 1⅜ in behind the track's center line, and add its brace behind it.
  3. Glue the servo to the post's front face with the shaft pointing toward you, about 2 in above the base, just left of the push-rod saddle (by the length of the arm).
  4. Push the long single arm onto the shaft, pointing at the far wall, as close to horizontal as the teeth allow.
  5. Straighten the paperclip. Hook one end through the arm's outermost hole and squeeze the hook closed; hook the top end through the saddle's hole the same way. Bend the rod until the beam sits close to level.

No glue on the rod's joints. They have to pivot.

4.2

Mount the sensors

  1. Push the ultrasonic sensor's cans through the plate's holes from outside, pins pointing down, and tape the board in place.
  2. Stick the GY-521 to the outside of the track near 6 in with double-sided tape. Any orientation works; calibration figures it out.
  3. Stick the LED matrix to the front tower below the skewer, facing you.
  4. Route the wires loosely so the beam swings without tugging on them.
4.3

Calibrate

  1. Put the ball on the track and click Calibrate (or press ST/REPT on the remote). It takes about 50 seconds.
  2. Sweep: the servo moves slowly end to end while the gyro measures the beam, giving a map from servo position to beam angle.
  3. Ball test: it tilts one way, back, and again, and watches which way the ball rolls.
  4. Auto-level: it holds the ball mid-track and nudges until it finds true level. The result is saved on the Arduino.

If it reports that the ball didn't roll, check that the ball is on the track and that the beam sits roughly level at rest (adjust the push-rod's bends), then calibrate again.

Part 5

Teach it

The robot can already balance with the starter brain I trained. Now train your own, examine it, and send it over.

5.1

Train a brain

  1. In the Dream tab, press Start training. Sixty-four simulated Fulcrums practice at once, each with different physics.
  2. Watch the learning curve: it climbs while the brain explores, then drops below the gray line, the score of a classic hand-tuned controller.
  3. Watch What the brain does: a clean diagonal forms as it learns that the right tilt depends on both where the ball is and how fast it rolls.
  4. Two or three minutes is plenty. Click the track to move the target, click the ball to flick it, or press You and try balancing it yourself.
5.2

The exam

  1. Open Exam, choose Best so far, and click Run the exam.
  2. It's graded on the 544-byte, 8-bit copy the Arduino will run. Passing most of the 32 worlds means it's ready.
5.3

Send the brain

  1. In Robot, click Send best brain. The Arduino checks every piece and stores the brain in memory that survives power-off.
  2. Choose Brain mode, set the ball on the track, and let go.
5.4

5.5

Dream vs reality

  1. In Robot, with Brain mode on, click Run the test. Fulcrum runs a 20-second routine of target jumps on the real track, then the Gym dreams the same routine with the same brain.
  2. The space between the blue and orange lines is the reality gap. Small gap: the dream was a good model of your cardboard.

The screenshot above is the virtual robot, whose physics are deliberately different from the dream's. Yours will tell you how good the simulator really is.

5.6

Experiments

  1. Skip the randomization. Turn off Train for the real world, train a new brain, send it. It aces its one perfect dream and struggles on real cardboard.
  2. Change the ball. Try a golf ball (pick it in the Gym first). Does the brain cope with a heavier, faster ball?
  3. Watch it learn from scratch. Press New brain and keep your eye on the decision map. The moment the diagonal appears is when it figured out balancing.
  4. Beat the classic controller. Run the dream-vs-reality test in Brain mode and in PID mode and compare.
  5. Be the brain. FUNC/STOP until the knob tilts the beam, and try to hold the ball still. Then let the brain have it back.

How it works, for the curious

The real equations behind the simulator, the learner and the robot.

The physics in the dream

Put the pivot at the origin, let s be the ball's position along the track measured from the pivot, θ the beam angle (positive tips the far end down), h the height of the ball's center above the pivot, rc the ball's rolling radius on the V, m and J its mass and moment of inertia. Rolling without slipping makes the ball's spin ω = ṡ/rc + θ̇, so

kinetic energy of the ball's center and its spin, minus gravity
differentiate the momentum along the track
centrifugal push and gravity's pull along the track
Euler–Lagrange, divided through by m + J/rc²

A ping-pong ball is a thin shell, J = ⅔mr², and in a 90° V it rolls on a radius rc = r/√2, so k = 1/(1 + 4/3) = 3/7 ≈ 0.43: only 43% of gravity's pull along the track becomes acceleration; the rest spins the ball. The heffθ̈ term is the kick the ball gets when the beam jerks. The simulator adds rolling friction that sticks below a threshold, bumps in the cardboard, a servo modeled as a stiff damped spring with dead time, speed and torque limits, gear slop, and an ultrasonic sensor with noise, dropouts, garbage echoes and a blind spot.

What the brain sees and does

The sonar's readings go through an alpha-beta tracker (the same arithmetic on the Arduino and in the dream):

The brain is a 6 → 16 → 16 → 1 network with tanh neurons. Its inputs are the miss e = x̂ − target (scaled by 15 cm), the speed (scaled by 40 cm/s), its last two tilts, the integral of the miss (scaled by 0.05 m·s), and where the ball is. Its output, between −1 and 1, is the tilt in units of 7°.

How it learns: PPO

Each practice step earns a reward for being close and calm:

Proximal Policy Optimization nudges the brain toward actions that turned out better than a second network, the critic V, expected, while never changing the policy too much at once:

The critic is allowed to peek at the dream's hidden physics (the true level error, the true ball position), which makes its estimates better; the brain that ships to the robot only ever sees the sensor. That split is called an asymmetric actor-critic. Training runs 64 worlds in parallel, 8,192 decisions per learning step.

Fitting it into 1 KB

Each layer's weights are stored as signed bytes with one scale per layer, w ≈ s·q, q = round(w/s), s = max|w|/127, so a neuron computes b + s·Σqkxk. The whole brain is 544 bytes with its header and CRC-32 checksum. The exam grades exactly this copy, and on a desktop the firmware's C++ matches the Gym's JavaScript to within 10−7.

Calibration without knowing how the gyro is mounted

Fulcrum reads which way is "up" (u) at the servo's middle and at both extremes (gA, gB). The pivot axis is n = (gA × gB)/|gA × gB|, and any later reading's beam angle is

which doesn't care which way the board faces. Level comes from the ball itself: a gentle controller holds it mid-track while a correction creeps toward wherever the ball drifts; averaged over ten seconds, the tilt that keeps the ball still is true level.

The real firmware was compiled for the Uno (27.9 KB of 32 KB flash, 790 bytes of RAM to spare) and also run on a Mac against the simulated robot: it calibrates, balances as well as the simulator predicts, accepts new brains and refuses a corrupted one. None of this is a promise about your cardboard. That's what the dream-vs-reality test is for.
